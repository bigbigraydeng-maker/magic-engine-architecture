In 2019, Auckland Council signed off the Drury-Opāheke Structure Plan — a formal, publicly notified framework for roughly 22,000 new homes, 60,000 new residents and 12,000 new jobs on land immediately north of Papakura. That is not sales copy; it is a council planning document. And it is no longer just a document sitting on a shelf. In August 2026, Drury and Paerātā rejoined Auckland's passenger rail network for the first time since 1972, with the City Rail Link opening weeks later. The wider corridor running from Takanini through Drury, Opāheke and Paerata to Pukekohe is projected to hold around 120,000 people within 30 years.

For anyone weighing up property in Rosehill or Papakura, that is the real backdrop worth understanding — what has actually been approved, what has actually been built, and what is still just a projection.

## What is the Drury-Opāheke Structure Plan, exactly?

A structure plan is Auckland Council's long-term blueprint for how a Future Urban Zone gets developed — it sets out where housing, business land, town centres, parks and infrastructure will go before individual developers apply for consents. Auckland Council adopted the Drury-Opāheke Structure Plan on 6 August 2019, following several years of public consultation that began in 2017.

The plan covers approximately 1,900 hectares directly north of Papakura and provides for, over roughly the next 30 years:

- **About 22,000 new homes**
- **About 60,000 new residents**
- **About 12,000 new jobs**, supported by roughly 245 hectares zoned for light industrial and business use
- **A new town centre** intended to anchor the area, rather than leaving it dependent on Papakura or Manukau

It sits alongside a parallel structure plan for Pukekohe-Paerata a little further south, which separately provides for around 12,500 homes and roughly 65,000 people by 2050. Taken together, Auckland Council has effectively pre-planned one of the largest single concentrations of new housing anywhere in the region, on the doorstep of Papakura and Rosehill.

## How does this fit into the bigger South Auckland picture?

Zoom out from Drury-Opāheke alone and the scale gets clearer. The corridor running from Takanini, through Drury and Opāheke, to Paerata and Pukekohe — about 20km south of the Auckland CBD — is projected by planners and reported by Local Democracy Reporting (via RNZ) to be home to around **120,000 people within 30 years**. That figure spans several structure plans and years of transport and infrastructure business cases, not one press release, which is why it is worth treating as directional rather than exact — but the direction is consistent across every source: this is where a large share of Auckland's next generation of housing is planned to land.

Rosehill and Papakura are not inside that Future Urban Zone. They sit right beside it, on the same road and rail corridor, which is the part that matters for anyone buying property there rather than in the growth area itself.

## Has any of this actually been built, or is it still just a plan?

This is the fair question to ask about any structure plan — councils adopt plans regularly, and not every one turns into steel and concrete on a useful timeframe. Here, some of it demonstrably has.

**Drury and Paerātā railway stations reopened to passengers on 2 August 2026** — restoring scheduled rail services to the area for the first time since the original stations closed in 1972. They were built by KiwiRail with government funding, sit on Auckland's Southern Line, and each provide 350 park-and-ride spaces with room for further expansion. Trains currently run early morning to late evening, seven days a week, connecting through to Auckland's city centre, Auckland Airport (via Puhinui and AirportLink) and Middlemore Hospital, with two new connecting bus services launched alongside them.

**Auckland's City Rail Link opens on 13 September 2026.** Once it does, the Southern Line through this corridor becomes the South-City Line, with trains running every 10 minutes at peak, every 15 minutes off-peak and every 30 minutes overnight — a material step up from the current timetable.

**A third station, Ngākōroa in Drury West, is under construction and planned to open in 2027**, which will put a station roughly midway through the growth area rather than only at its edges.

None of that is a promise about what will happen by 2050. It is a record of what has already opened, with dates, funding sources and specifications that are a matter of public record — which is a genuinely different thing from a structure plan diagram.

## Where does Rosehill actually sit in this?

Rosehill is a suburb within the Papakura Local Board area, which Auckland Council describes as extending between Alfriston and Drury and taking in Takanini, Hingaia, Red Hill, Pahurehure and the Papakura town centre. In practical terms, Rosehill sits at the southern edge of the growth corridor described above — sharing the same Southern Line, the same state highway network, and increasingly the same commuting catchment as Drury, without itself being rezoned Future Urban land.

That distinction matters for a buyer. Land inside a Future Urban Zone comes with a longer runway — structure planning, then rezoning, then subdivision, then services, before homes are consented at scale. Rosehill is already an established, fully serviced residential area within the Papakura Local Board, positioned to pick up the transport and employment benefit of the corridor without carrying that same development lead time.

## How fast is the local population already growing?

Growth in this part of Auckland is not only a 30-year projection — it is already showing up in the numbers. Stats NZ recorded a population of 57,636 in the Papakura Local Board area at the 2018 census. By the 2023 census that had reached 72,318 — an increase of 14,682 people, or 25.5%, in five years. The latest subnational population estimate puts the Papakura Local Board area at **84,200 people as of June 2025**.

That is a local board area that has grown by close to half since 2018, well before the new Drury stations or the City Rail Link had opened. It is one of the more concrete, checkable signals available on how real the demand behind this corridor already is.

## What does this mean if you're weighing up buying in Rosehill?

We are a developer, not a financial adviser, so treat what follows as context rather than advice — and do your own research, or take independent advice, before acting on it.

What can be said factually: Rosehill sits directly against a corridor where Auckland Council has committed to a structure plan since 2019, where the population has already grown by a quarter in five years, and where the single biggest piece of promised infrastructure — passenger rail — has now actually opened rather than remaining a line on a map. Those are three different, independently verifiable facts pointing the same direction. They do not guarantee any particular property outcome, and buyers should weigh them alongside their own budget, timeframe and independent advice — but they are a genuinely different starting point from a suburb where "growth" is only ever discussed in a sales brochure.

If you are researching new-build options in the area, the same checks apply here as anywhere: confirm consent status, understand who is actually building it and under what licence, and read the sale and purchase agreement — including the sunset clause and specification schedule — before you sign.
